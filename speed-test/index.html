    How it works

    Three short phases, real network traffic, no fake numbers.

    1. 01

      Latency

      We send 20 tiny requests to the nearest edge server and measure the round-trip time of each one. The first request is discarded as a warm-up.

      Median ping + average jitter

    2. 02

      Throughput

      Several parallel streams pull progressively larger files for about 10 seconds. The first seconds are skipped so TCP slow start doesn't skew the result.

      4–6 parallel connections

    3. 03

      Scoring

      Your result is compared against what common online activities actually need, so the verdict is meaningful rather than a raw number.

      Plain-language rating

    Frequently asked questions

    Everything people usually want to know before trusting a speed test.

    Why is my result lower than the speed my ISP advertises?

    Advertised speeds are the maximum of the line, measured under ideal conditions with no other traffic. Real results are always lower because some bandwidth is used by your router, Wi-Fi, the TV streaming in the background, the VPN you're connected to, and by the fact that the internet is a shared network that peaks at different times.

    As a rule of thumb, a healthy result lands at 80–95% of the advertised number on Ethernet, and noticeably lower on Wi-Fi.

    Should I use Wi-Fi or Ethernet?

    Use Ethernet for the most accurate reading. Wi-Fi shares airtime with every other device on the network, and 2.4 GHz bands are especially susceptible to interference from neighbouring networks.

    If you must use Wi-Fi, stand close to the router, use the 5 GHz or 6 GHz band, and close other devices that are streaming or downloading.

    What is jitter and why does it matter?

    Jitter is the variation in your latency. Imagine a ping of 20 ms that never changes: that's steady. Now imagine a ping that jumps between 5 ms and 90 ms: the average might still look fine, but every second of a video call stutters and online games rubber-band.

    For calls and gaming, jitter matters more than the average ping. Under 10 ms is excellent, under 30 ms is fine.

    What is Mbps, and do I need more?

    Mbps means megabits per second — a measure of how much data moves each second. One megabit is roughly 125 KB, so 100 Mbps downloads about 12.5 MB per second.

    As a rough guide: 5 Mbps handles one HD stream, 25 Mbps handles a 4K stream plus browsing, 100 Mbps comfortably covers a busy household, and 300 Mbps or more matters if you work from home, game competitively, or upload large files.

    How can I improve my speed?

    Start with the boring stuff: connect by Ethernet, move closer to the router, use the 5 GHz band, and reboot the router to clear any degraded state. Then close apps that sync in the background — cloud backups, video calls and large downloads all compete for the same pipe.

    Also check that your device isn't the bottleneck: old Wi-Fi cards, USB network adapters and power-saving modes on the Wi-Fi radio all cap you well below what the line can deliver.

    Which server is my test running against?

    Cloudflare routes each request to its nearest data centre — an "edge" location. The results panel shows which one answered, and the distance to it, so you can tell whether latency reflects a busy local network or a genuinely distant server.
